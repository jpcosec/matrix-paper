# 2. Motivation: Why Embeddings Are Not Enough

Modern Large Language Models represent semantic meaning as points within high-dimensional continuous spaces. While this enables powerful generalization and fuzzy matching, it introduces structural vulnerabilities that preclude formal guarantees. The geometry of such spaces fundamentally conflates statistical proximity with logical validity. For instance, in a typical word embedding, "apples" and "oranges" occupy a similar neighborhood due to shared features such as being edible fruit. However, statistical proximity does not translate to propositional truth or categorical distinctness. More critically, continuous spaces lack discrete boundaries or "out-of-bounds" conditions; any arbitrary prompt can generate a valid vector, even if the resulting combination—such as "the square root of an apple"—constitutes a clear category error. 

We contend that the pervasive issue of "hallucination" is actually a structural failure of representability. Without an inherent grammar of sense to reject nonsensical combinations of valid signs, the system remains incapable of identifying what Wittgenstein termed *Unsinn* (nonsense). Furthermore, the reliance on a global embedding space leads to "flat" ontologies where truth values are averaged over conflicting contexts. A proposition valid in a culinary context (e.g., "tomatoes are vegetables") may be botanically false, yet continuous embeddings struggle to maintain these strict discursive boundaries. This leads to semantic leakage across unrelated domains, where the rules of one world are incorrectly applied to another, rendering the model's assertions untrustworthy for formal applications.
